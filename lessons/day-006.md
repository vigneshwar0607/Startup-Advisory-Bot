# Startup Advisory — Day 6: Founders' Agreements, Vesting, and Leaver Clauses

_2026-11-19_

## 1. Today in one line
Reverse vesting paired with clear good/bad leaver clauses prevents deadweight equity from crippling an early-stage startup's cap table.

## 2. The concept
When founders incorporate a private limited company, they typically subscribe to equity shares at face value (for example, INR 10 or INR 1 per share) to own 100% of the initial paid-up capital. If two co-founders take a 50:50 split on Day 1 without restrictions, both own their shares outright. If Founder B quits after four months to take a corporate job, Founder B still legally owns 50% of the equity. No serious angel investor or venture capital fund will invest in a company where an inactive former member holds a large equity stake.

To resolve this, standard startup structuring uses **reverse vesting**:
1. **Upfront Issuance:** Founders subscribe to the shares immediately so that voting rights, dividend rights, and long-term capital asset holding periods start running on Day 1.
2. **Contractual Restriction:** The Founders' Agreement (and later, the Articles of Association) subjects these shares to a vesting schedule. The market standard is a 4-year vesting schedule with a 1-year cliff.
   - **The Cliff:** If a founder leaves within the first 12 months, 0% has vested.
   - **Subsequent Vesting:** After Month 12, 25% vests immediately, and the remaining 75% vests monthly or quarterly in equal tranches over the remaining 36 months.
3. **Repurchase Right:** If a founder exits before full vesting, the unvested shares must be transferred back. 

The treatment of shares upon departure depends on the leaver status:
- **Good Leaver:** Departure caused by death, permanent incapacitation, termination without cause, or a departure mutually agreed upon by all founders. The good leaver retains 100% of their vested shares. Unvested shares are surrendered or repurchased at face value or original subscription cost.
- **Bad Leaver:** Departure caused by voluntary resignation without co-founder approval, material breach of the Founders' Agreement, criminal conduct, or fraud. The bad leaver forfeits all unvested shares at face value. In strict agreements, the remaining founders also receive a call option to acquire the bad leaver's *vested* shares at a discount (often the lower of fair market value and subscription price).

Crucially, every Founders' Agreement must contain an **Intellectual Property (IP) Assignment Clause**. This ensures that any code, designs, business plans, or domain names created by any founder belong unconditionally to the company, not to the individual.

## 3. Practical CA angle
Founders often come to a CA after incorporating via an online portal without any Founders' Agreement in place. This provides an immediate advisory entry point.

- **The Service:** Structuring the founders' equity arrangement, advising on reverse vesting mechanics, identifying tax and corporate law risks, and drafting or reviewing the Founders' Agreement alongside legal counsel.
- **Typical Deliverables:**
  1. Founders' Agreement / Co-founders' Term Sheet.
  2. Vesting schedule matrix tied to cap table models.
  3. Documentation amending the Articles of Association (AoA) to incorporate vesting restrictions (agreements between shareholders are difficult to enforce against the company unless reflected in the AoA).
- **Indicative Fee Range:** INR 25,000 to INR 60,000 (clearly marked as indicative, varying with negotiation complexity and cross-border founder involvement).

## 5. Watch-outs
- **The Section 68 Buyback Trap:** Do not structure the Founders' Agreement so that the *company* buys back unvested shares. Under Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013, a company can only buy back shares out of free reserves, securities premium, or fresh issue proceeds, and must comply with debt-equity ratios and solvency filings. Seed startups have zero reserves. Instead, structure the repurchase as a **Call Option held by the remaining founder(s)** or an Employee Welfare Trust.
- **Tax Implications on Transfer below FMV:** Under the Income-tax Act, 1961 (and the incoming Income-tax Act, 2025 framework starting 1 April 2026), transferring shares between founders at face value when the fair market value (FMV) is higher can trigger deemed income in the hands of the recipient under Section 56(2)(x) (or its 2025 Act equivalent), and capital gains computation under Section 50CA based on Rule 11UA valuation. While nominal transfers at early pre-seed stage often equal FMV, this becomes a severe tax exposure once the startup has raised external rounds at a premium.
- **Unamended Articles of Association (AoA):** A private agreement between shareholders that restricts share transfer does not bind the company unless those terms are altered into the company's AoA under Section 14 of the Companies Act, 2013.

## 7. Quick quiz
1. Why is an upfront equity issuance with reverse vesting preferred in India over issuing shares gradually over four years?
2. Why should the call option for unvested shares be granted to remaining co-founders rather than the startup company itself?
3. What is the standard duration of the "cliff" in a 4-year vesting schedule?

### Answers
1. Issuing shares upfront fixes the subscription price at face value, starts the capital gains holding period immediately, avoids future valuation complications, and grants immediate voting rights.
2. Direct buyback by the company requires compliance with Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013, which mandates available free reserves that early-stage startups lack.
3. 12 months (1 year). If a founder leaves before 12 months, zero equity vests.

**Concept: e₹ Wallets, Tokens, and Denominations**
The Digital Rupee (e₹) is a sovereign digital bearer token issued by the Reserve Bank of India. It does not earn interest and represents a direct liability of the RBI. Unlike commercial bank money (which consists of deposit balances held across individual bank balance sheets), e₹ is issued in fixed digital token denominations identical to physical currency notes and coins (e.g., ₹0.50, ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10, ₹20, ₹50, ₹100, ₹200, ₹500). Users store these tokens in a digital token wallet hosted on mobile devices through authorized banks. Transactions occur by transferring cryptographic tokens directly from one wallet to another, achieving real-time settlement without commercial bank intermediation.

**Why it matters to a CA:** Corporate e₹ wallets will require specific balance sheet disclosures and independent treasury audit verification protocols distinct from traditional bank accounts.
